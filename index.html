How the peg pyramid works

The board is a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row one peg wider than the row above it. A ball dropped at the top has an even chance of moving left or right at each point, then repeats that split through every row it crosses. After the last peg, it lands in one of the bottom pockets — always one more pocket than the number of rows.

Because each path is built from repeated fifty-fifty turns, most balls settle near the middle while only a few reach the outer edges. Mathematicians call this a Galton board, and the pattern it creates follows a binomial curve. In practical terms, the centre pays less often but more often hits, while the outer pockets are rare and carry stronger multipliers. Six words worth knowing

Pocket
A slot at the bottom of the board that holds the multiplier earned by the final ball position.
Rows
The number of peg lines a ball crosses; more rows mean more pockets and sharper odds.
Risk level
A preset that stretches or compresses the payout table without changing the long-run return much.
House margin
The small difference between fair odds and paid odds that supports the operator.
Provably fair
A method using published hashes so players can verify that a round was not altered.
Variance
The size of the swings around the average result — the part the risk switch controls most.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each deflection comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you confirm afterwards that the result was set before the ball moved.

Do more rows improve the payout?

No. More rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge prizes become larger while their probability falls. The overall return stays broadly the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is best?

None is mathematically superior. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk concentrates value into rare edge hits. The right choice depends on how much swing your budget can handle.
